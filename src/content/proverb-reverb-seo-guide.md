## The 5 Essential Types of Reverb Every Producer Must Know

Understanding the core types of reverb is crucial before adding spatial effects to your tracks. Different reverb styles serve distinct mixing purposes:

### 1. Algorithmic Reverb
Instead of playing back static audio samples, **algorithmic reverbs** use complex mathematical delay networks (such as Feedback Delay Networks, or FDN) to calculate and synthesize sound reflections in real time.
* **Sound Character:** Lush, smooth, unnatural, and cinematic.
* **Best Used On:** Lead vocals, modern synths, pads, and rhythmic spatial automation.
* **Why It Dominates Modern Mixes:** Algorithmic reverbs offer total parameter control over early reflections, decay time, modulation, diffusion, and stereo width.

### 2. Convolution Reverb
Convolution reverbs use **Impulse Responses (IRs)**—audio recordings of real physical spaces (like cathedrals, stairwells, or vintage hardware units) captured with microphones—to recreate exact real-world acoustic profiles.
* **Sound Character:** Hyper-realistic and natural.
* **Drawback:** Heavy CPU consumption and limited real-time sound design flexibility.

### 3. Plate Reverb
Originally invented in the 1950s using a massive 600-pound suspended steel sheet vibrating from an audio transducer (like the iconic EMT 140), **plate reverb** creates a dense, bright, metallic initial burst without discrete room reflections.
* **Best Used On:** Pop and Rock lead vocals, snare drums, and acoustic guitars.

### 4. Hall & Cathedral Reverb
Hall reverbs simulate massive concert halls, scoring stages, and churches with long decay times (typically 2.5 to 8+ seconds).
* **Best Used On:** Cinematic orchestral strings, ambient pads, background backing vocal beds, and piano solos.

### 5. Spring Reverb
Spring reverbs pass an audio signal through physical coiled metal springs. They produce a characteristic dark, boingy, metallic resonance.
* **Best Used On:** Vintage electric guitars, reggae dub snares, and lo-fi keys.

## How to Mix Vocals with Reverb: 3 Essential Pro Rules

Follow these three studio-tested techniques to dial in professional, radio-ready vocal space using ProVerb:

### Step 1: Always Calculate Tempo-Synced Pre-Delay
Pre-delay is the gap in milliseconds between the initial dry vocal and the onset of the reverb tail. If pre-delay is set to 0 ms, the reverb immediately attaches to the consonants, pushing the vocal backward into the mix.
To lock your pre-delay to your song's BPM, use this formula:
$$\text{Pre-Delay (ms)} = \frac{60,000}{\text{BPM}} \times \frac{1}{64} \text{ or } \frac{1}{32}$$
*For a 120 BPM track, a 1/64-note pre-delay is approximately 15.6 ms. This tiny gap ensures your vocal transients hit the listener's ear with maximum punch before the acoustic space blooms.*

### Step 2: Apply the "Abbey Road" Reverb Filter Trick
Legendary Abbey Road engineers rarely sent full-bandwidth signals into their plate reverbs. Inside ProVerb's Modern Reverb EQ:
1. Set the **Low Cut (High Pass Filter) to 120 Hz – 180 Hz** to remove rumble and mud.
2. Set the **High Cut (Low Pass Filter) to 6,000 Hz – 8,000 Hz** to eliminate harsh sibilance ("S" and "T" sounds) from creating distracting metallic reflections.

### Step 3: Dial In 25% to 35% Vocal Ducking
Turn up ProVerb's **Ducking Amount to 30%**. This transparently ducks the reverb by -4 dB to -6 dB during active vocal phrases, keeping every syllable distinct and intelligible while maintaining an expensive, cinematic tail between lyrical gaps.
